Climbing terms can make a first gym visit sound like a different language. “Beta” is advice, a “send” is a successful climb, and “take” asks a belayer to tighten the rope—not to grab the wall.

This glossary covers the words you are most likely to hear indoors. Facility rules and belay commands can vary, so use the exact language taught by your gym.

## Essential Climbing Terms at a Glance

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
|---|---|
| Beta | Information about how to climb a route |
| Send | Complete a climb without falling or weighting the rope |
| Project | A climb you are working toward completing |
| Crux | The hardest move or sequence |
| Flash | Complete on the first try after receiving information |
| Onsight | Complete on the first try without prior information |
| Belay | Manage the rope for a climber |
| Take | Remove slack and hold the climber on the rope |
| Slack | Give the climber more rope |
| Dyno | A dynamic move where the body leaves the wall |

## Types of Indoor Climbing

**Bouldering** is climbing short problems over padded flooring without ropes. A boulder usually has a marked start and finish. Read [what bouldering is](/blog/what-is-bouldering/) before your first session.

**Top rope** means the rope runs from the climber to an anchor above and back to the belayer. The belayer manages slack from the ground. Our [top-rope guide](/blog/top-rope-climbing-guide/) explains the format.

**Lead climbing** means the climber clips the rope into protection points while ascending. USA Climbing's [lead discipline overview](https://usaclimbing.org/climb/lead/) describes competitors clipping quickdraws as they move up the wall. Indoor lead climbing requires specific instruction and gym authorization.

**Auto-belay** is a device that takes in line as a climber ascends and controls the descent. It does not eliminate the need for the gym's connection check; see our [auto-belay guide](/blog/auto-belay-climbing-guide/).

**Speed climbing** is a timed race on a standardized route. Learn the format in our [speed climbing guide](/blog/speed-climbing-guide/).

## Route and Attempt Terms

**Route** usually describes a roped climb. **Problem** or **boulder** usually describes a bouldering climb.

**Set** means install or design a climb. A **route setter** chooses the holds and intended movement.

**Beta** is information about the sequence. Unasked-for advice is often called **beta spraying**. Ask before explaining someone else's project.

**Project** is a climb you have not completed yet but are actively attempting.

**Crux** is the hardest part for a particular climber. It may be one move, a rest position, or a sustained section.

**Send** means complete the climb under the accepted rules. **Redpoint** usually means send after prior attempts. **Flash** means send first try with prior information; **onsight** means first try without prior information.

**Attempt** starts when you leave the ground. In casual gym climbing, people use the word loosely. Competitions define starts and completions more precisely.

**Top** means control the finish as required. On many boulders, that means controlling the marked final hold with both hands. Follow local rules.

## Climbing Movement Terms

**Flag:** Extend one leg to counterbalance and stop the hips from rotating away.

**Drop knee:** Turn a knee inward and lower it to bring the hip closer to the wall.

**Heel hook:** Place the heel on a hold and pull or stabilize with the back of the leg.

**Toe hook:** Pull against a hold with the top of the toe or shoe.

**Mantle:** Press down on a hold to bring the body above it, like getting out of a pool.

**Layback:** Pull with the hands in one direction while pushing with the feet in the opposite direction.

**Stem:** Push the feet against opposing surfaces to support the body.

**Smear:** Press the shoe rubber against the wall where there is no clear foothold.

**Dyno:** Jump or launch so all points of contact may leave the wall. A **deadpoint** is a more controlled dynamic reach timed near the top of the body's motion.

**Campus:** Climb without using the feet. It also describes training on a campus board. This is demanding; see the [campus board workout guide](/blog/campus-board-workout/) before treating it as casual practice.

Practice these deliberately with our [climbing technique guide](/blog/how-to-improve-climbing-technique/) and [route-reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-climbing-routes-before-you-climb/).

## Hold and Grip Terms

**Jug:** A large, positive hold that is easy to wrap the fingers around.

**Crimp:** A small edge. “Crimp” can describe the hold or a grip position. Do not assume every small edge requires the most closed grip.

**Sloper:** A rounded hold relying on contact, body position, and friction rather than a deep lip.

**Pinch:** A hold squeezed between the thumb and fingers.

**Pocket:** A hole used by one or more fingers. Use conservative loading on unfamiliar pockets.

**Undercling:** A hold pulled upward from below.

**Sidepull:** A hold pulled sideways.

**Gaston:** A sideways push with the elbow out, like opening elevator doors.

**Volume:** A large geometric feature attached to the wall. Holds may be mounted on it, and the volume itself may be usable unless the gym says otherwise.

## Rope and Gear Terms

**Harness:** Worn around the waist and legs to connect a climber or belayer to the rope system.

**Belay device:** Equipment used to manage rope and help arrest a fall. Devices differ; use only one you have been taught to operate.

**Carabiner:** A metal connector with a gate. A **locking carabiner** has an added locking mechanism.

**Quickdraw:** Two carabiners joined by a sling, used as protection points in lead climbing.

**Chalk:** Magnesium carbonate used to manage moisture on the hands. Gym rules may limit loose or colored chalk.

**Climbing shoes:** Close-fitting shoes with sticky rubber designed for footholds. See [how to choose climbing shoes](/blog/how-to-choose-climbing-shoes/).

## Common Climbing Commands

These are communication words, not a self-teaching belay lesson. Take the gym's orientation and use its approved sequence.

- **On belay?** The climber asks whether the belayer is ready.
- **Belay on.** The belayer confirms the system and readiness.
- **Climbing.** The climber announces the start.
- **Climb on.** The belayer confirms.
- **Take.** The climber asks the belayer to remove slack and support them.
- **Slack.** The climber asks for more rope.
- **Lower.** The climber asks to be lowered after both people confirm readiness.
- **Off belay.** Used only within the taught procedure and context.

Names matter in a noisy gym. Use the climber's name with a command when multiple rope teams are nearby.

## Grades and Gym Language

**Grade** estimates difficulty. U.S. bouldering gyms often use the V scale; roped gyms often use the Yosemite Decimal System. Other regions and gyms use different systems. Convert common systems with our [bouldering grade tool](/tools/bouldering-grade-conversion/) and [sport-climbing grade tool](/tools/sport-climbing-grade-conversion/).

**Soft** means a climb feels easier than its posted grade; **stiff** or **sandbagged** means it feels harder. These are opinions, not measurements.

**Style** describes the movement a climb favors—powerful, technical, slabby, steep, reachy, or sustained. A grade does not say whether the style suits you.

**Pump** is the familiar forearm fatigue and loss of force during sustained climbing. **Shake out** means relax and move an arm to recover at a rest.

## FAQ

### What does beta mean in climbing?

Beta is information about how to climb a route or problem: hold order, feet, body position, or a specific move. Ask before giving it.

### What is a send?

A send is a successful completion without falling or resting on the rope. The exact start and finish rules depend on the gym or competition.

### What does “take” mean?

It asks the belayer to remove slack and hold the climber on the rope. Use the exact commands taught by your facility.

### What is the difference between a flash and an onsight?

Both are first-try completions. A flash allows prior information; an onsight does not.

### Where can I practice these terms?

Use the [gym search](/search/) to find a facility, then take its required orientation. Vocabulary helps you understand the room; staff instruction teaches the actual procedures.

## Bottom Line

Learn the terms that affect safety first: climbing formats, equipment, and commands. Then add movement and route language as you encounter it. You do not need to sound like a veteran on day one—you need to communicate clearly and follow the gym's rules.
