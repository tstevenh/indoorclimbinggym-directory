A climbing core workout should help you keep force moving between hands and feet. It is not a contest to hold the longest plank. The useful qualities are body tension, anti-rotation, controlled hip movement, and the ability to keep feet engaged when the wall gets steep.

Two short sessions per week are enough for most indoor climbers to train those qualities without stealing time from climbing. Use the plan below for six to eight weeks, progress one variable at a time, and keep the reps clean.

## Quick climbing core workout

Alternate these two 20–25 minute sessions. Leave at least one day between them.

### Session A: tension and anti-extension

1. Dead bug: 3 sets of 6–10 controlled reps per side
2. Hollow-body hold or tuck hold: 3 sets of 15–30 seconds
3. Body saw or long-lever plank: 3 sets of 6–10 reps
4. Hanging knee raise: 3 sets of 5–10 reps
5. Easy steep-wall foot-on drill: 3 rounds of 45–75 seconds

### Session B: rotation control and hips

1. Side plank: 3 sets of 15–30 seconds per side
2. Pallof press: 3 sets of 8–12 reps per side
3. Bird dog: 3 sets of 6–10 reps per side
4. Controlled windshield-wiper regression: 3 sets of 4–8 reps per side
5. Easy traversing with deliberate hip turns: 3 rounds of 60–90 seconds

Stop each set when your position changes. More time in a poor shape is not useful volume.

## What “core” means in climbing

The trunk rarely works alone on the wall. One foot pushes. The opposite hand pulls. The pelvis wants to rotate away. Your trunk has to transmit force while the hips reposition for the next move.

That creates four practical demands:

- **Anti-extension:** keeping the ribs and pelvis connected instead of sagging away from the wall.
- **Anti-rotation:** resisting unwanted twisting when holds pull you in different directions.
- **Controlled rotation:** turning hips deliberately for drop knees, reaches, and flagging.
- **Hip-to-rib connection:** keeping feet useful while the upper body moves.

This is why hundreds of rushed sit-ups are a poor default. They train movement, but not necessarily the shapes and force transfer that climbing exposes.

A climbing-specific study compared dynamic and isometric core programs in 19 advanced and elite climbers over ten weeks. Both groups improved climbing-specific core test performance, and neither approach was clearly superior overall. The authors supported including both dynamic and isometric work. Read the [full study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6179192/) for the protocol and limitations.

## The eight best core exercises for climbers

“Best” means easy to scale, simple to repeat, and relevant to a wall position. It does not mean every exercise belongs in every session.

### 1. Dead bug

Lie on your back with hips and knees bent. Keep the ribs controlled while you extend the opposite arm and leg. Return before the lower back loses its position.

Why it helps: it teaches limb movement without losing trunk position, a basic version of moving one hand or foot while the rest of the body stays organized.

Progress by lengthening the lever or slowing the rep, not by rushing.

### 2. Hollow-body hold

Use a tucked shape first. Flatten the torso into the floor, then extend arms or legs only as far as you can maintain the position.

Why it helps: the hollow shape resembles the front-side tension used to keep feet from cutting on steep terrain.

If your lower back arches hard, shorten the lever.

### 3. Body saw

Start in a forearm plank with feet on sliders or socks on a smooth floor. Move the body backward a small distance, then pull forward while keeping the trunk rigid.

Why it helps: it adds a changing lever to anti-extension work. That is more demanding than holding the same plank shape indefinitely.

### 4. Side plank

Hold a straight line from shoulder through hip to foot, or use a bent-knee version. Do not let the top hip roll backward.

Why it helps: side-body tension supports flagging, compression, and positions where one foot has to counter a pull from the opposite hand.

### 5. Pallof press

Stand or kneel beside a cable or resistance band. Press the handle away from your chest without rotating toward the anchor.

Why it helps: the force tries to twist you, so the trunk learns to resist rotation while the arms move.

### 6. Bird dog

From hands and knees, extend the opposite arm and leg without shifting the pelvis. Pause, return, and switch sides.

Why it helps: it trains cross-body control with a low entry cost. It is a good choice on easier days.

### 7. Hanging knee raise

Hang from a bar with shoulders active, then raise the knees without swinging. Lower under control.

Why it helps: it connects grip, shoulders, trunk, and hips. But the grip cost matters—do not put high-volume hanging work before a hard climbing session.

### 8. Foot-on wall tension drill

Choose easy holds on a moderately steep wall. Keep both feet on while moving slowly between large handholds. Pause whenever the feet begin to peel, reset the hips, and continue.

Why it helps: transfer happens on the wall. This drill forces the trunk work to coordinate with actual feet and holds.

Use [Climbing Footwork Drills](/blog/climbing-footwork-drills/) if the problem is where you place your feet rather than whether you can keep tension through them.

## How hard should a climbing core workout be?

Most sets should finish with one or two good reps—or several good seconds—still available. Core training fails when fatigue changes the task:

- the lower back arches
- hips rotate or sag
- shoulders collapse
- momentum replaces control
- breathing stops for the entire set

When that happens, end the set or use an easier variation.

Progress in this order:

1. Improve position.
2. Add a few seconds or reps.
3. Lengthen the lever.
4. Add resistance.
5. Use a more climbing-specific variation.

Do not increase all five at once.

## A six-week progression

### Weeks 1–2: learn the shapes

Use the easiest version of each exercise. Keep holds near 15–20 seconds and dynamic sets around six controlled reps per side. The goal is repeatability.

### Weeks 3–4: add volume

Add five seconds to holds or two reps per set. Keep the same exercise variation unless every set stays clean.

### Weeks 5–6: increase leverage or resistance

Choose one progression per exercise: a longer hollow position, straighter legs, a stronger band, or a slightly longer wall drill. Do not turn the whole session into a maximal test.

After six weeks, repeat the original versions. If they feel easier and your wall drills are more controlled, the block did its job. If only your plank time improved, add more climbing-specific tension work in the next block.

## How to schedule core training around climbing

Core work fits best after climbing or general strength training, not before a session where you need maximum coordination.

A simple three-day climbing week could look like this:

| Day | Main work | Core |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Hard bouldering | Session A, reduced to 3–4 exercises |
| Wednesday | Technique or easier routes | None or bird dog/side plank only |
| Friday | Routes or strength | Session B |
| Weekend | Rest or easy climbing | Optional mobility, no catch-up workout |

If your weekly plan already feels crowded, use [How to Structure a Climbing Week](/blog/how-to-structure-climbing-week-strength-technique-recovery/) and the [Training Load & Recovery Planner](/tools/training-load-recovery-planner/) before adding another session.

## What core strength cannot fix

Core training is popular because almost every movement can be described as “body tension.” That does not mean weak core strength is always the limiter.

A 2023 [systematic review of climbing performance tests](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10203485/) found limited validity evidence for many climbing core tests. Some tests were reliable, but correlations with climbing ability varied. That is a useful warning: better test scores do not automatically equal better climbing.

Core work will not fix:

- feet placed on the wrong part of a hold
- poor route reading
- fear-driven overgripping
- finger strength that is genuinely below the route demand
- insufficient endurance for long sequences
- trying moves that you have not practiced technically

Use [How to Improve Climbing Technique](/blog/how-to-improve-climbing-technique/) and [Climbing Strength Training](/blog/climbing-strength-training/) to separate those problems.

## FAQ

### How often should climbers train core?

Two short sessions per week is a practical starting point. Experienced athletes may tolerate more, but the work should not reduce climbing quality or recovery.

### Should I do core before or after climbing?

Usually after. Fatiguing the trunk before hard climbing can reduce body control. A brief low-effort activation is different from a full workout.

### Are planks good for climbing?

They can be, especially as an entry-level isometric exercise. Progress toward side planks, changing levers, anti-rotation, and wall drills rather than chasing very long holds.

### Do hanging leg raises improve climbing?

They train a useful connection between grip, shoulders, trunk, and hips, but they are not a guaranteed grade booster. Use controlled reps and account for the extra grip load.

### Can core training stop my feet from cutting loose?

It can help when poor trunk tension is the cause. Feet may also cut because of hold choice, hip position, timing, finger capacity, or simply trying a move that requires a deliberate cut.

## Bottom line

Train the core to control force, not to collect exercise records. Combine isometric shapes, controlled dynamic reps, anti-rotation, and actual wall tension drills. Two focused sessions are enough to learn whether the work transfers.
