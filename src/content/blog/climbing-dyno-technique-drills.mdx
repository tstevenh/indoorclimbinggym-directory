A climbing dyno is a dynamic move in which momentum carries you to a hold that is difficult or impossible to reach statically. Good dyno technique is not a blind jump. It coordinates the feet, hips, arms, eyes, and timing so the body travels toward the target instead of peeling away from the wall.

Practice on easy, well-padded problems with a clear landing. Start with moves you can touch comfortably and increase distance only when takeoff and landing stay controlled.

## Quick Answer: How Do You Do a Climbing Dyno?

1. Identify the target, useful footholds, and safe landing zone.
2. Set the feet and bring the hips into a position that allows a controlled dip.
3. Look at the target hold before initiating the move.
4. Drive through the legs and hips while pulling just enough with the arms to keep the body close.
5. Release the starting holds at the right moment rather than hanging on until momentum disappears.
6. Reach for the target near the deadpoint, when upward speed is briefly lowest.
7. Absorb the catch with the shoulders, elbows, core, and feet when possible.
8. If you miss, land with bent knees on a clear mat and reset instead of flailing for the wall.

This sequence is a model, not a promise that every dyno uses the same body position. Wall angle, footholds, target direction, hold shape, and your reach change the solution.

## What Makes a Dyno Work?

### Force starts at the feet

Many failed dynos look like pull-ups with a small jump added. The legs and hips should create most of the displacement. Arms help connect the body to the wall and guide the path, but pulling early can compress the movement and send the hips away from the wall.

### The hips follow a path

Think about moving your center of mass toward the target line. If the takeoff pushes straight away from the wall, reaching farther with the hands will not repair the trajectory.

A biomechanics study comparing dyno techniques found that body position and countermovement affected vertical displacement in the [dyno maneuver](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19346182.2015.1106549). The practical lesson is simple: the setup and dip are part of the move, not wasted motion before it.

### The catch happens at the deadpoint

The deadpoint is the brief part of the trajectory where upward velocity approaches zero before the body begins to fall. Reaching the hold there reduces the speed you must control at contact.

Do not freeze after contact. Continue engaging through the shoulders and core, and bring the feet back to the wall when the problem allows it.

## A Safe Dyno Practice Progression

### Drill 1: Controlled rock and release

Choose large handholds and stable footholds on a vertical or mildly overhanging wall. Rock the hips down and up without releasing. Feel when the legs are driving and when the arms are merely guiding.

Then briefly release one hand at the top of the movement and return it to the same hold. The goal is timing, not distance.

### Drill 2: Static-to-dynamic contrast

Pick a hold you can reach statically. Touch it once with a slow reach, then repeat with a small coordinated pop. Use less pulling and notice how the feet change the effort.

This creates a low-consequence way to compare movement strategies. It also keeps ego out of the distance decision.

### Drill 3: Two-hand target catch

Use a large target hold only slightly beyond static reach. Drive with both feet and catch with both hands. Keep the target close enough that a miss produces a normal mat landing rather than a long swing.

Stop the drill when catches become straight-armed, off-axis, or desperate. Quality disappears before motivation does.

### Drill 4: Direction changes

Once a straight upward catch is repeatable, practice small left, right, and diagonal targets. Keep the same distance while learning to aim the hips, not just the hands.

### Drill 5: Link the move into climbing

Place the dyno inside an easy problem with moves before and after it. Approach rhythm, foot choice, and the move after the catch often matter more than a single isolated jump.

For more movement work, combine this with the [climbing footwork drills](/blog/climbing-footwork-drills/) and [route-reading system](/blog/how-to-read-climbing-routes-before-you-climb/).

## Common Dyno Mistakes

### Pulling before the legs drive

Early pulling brings the chest toward the starting holds but can limit the hip extension that creates distance. Keep the arms connected without turning the move into a maximal pull-up.

### Looking away from the target

The eyes help organize the movement. Choose the exact contact area before takeoff and keep looking at it through the catch.

### Jumping away from the wall

This usually begins with the hip position or foot pressure. Reduce the distance and practice a path that stays close enough for the hands to meet the hold.

### Catching with rigid elbows

A locked, uncontrolled catch can create a harsh swing. Use an easier target, arrive closer to the deadpoint, and absorb the load through a coordinated upper body.

### Practicing while exhausted

Dynos depend on timing. When the legs stop driving, the fingers miss the target, or landings become careless, the useful practice is over. Switch to easier movement or end the session.

## How Strong Do You Need to Be?

Strength helps, but dynos are not a single strength test. Leg power, hip trajectory, dynamic pulling, finger force, contact timing, shoulder control, confidence, and hold choice all contribute.

Research comparing climbing disciplines reported that boulder climbers produced higher average velocity in a dynamic pull-up test than lead climbers in the studied groups. The difference was [26.2%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6752829/), but that does not create a required score for recreational climbers.

A systematic review and meta-analysis found that climbing-specific finger training improved rate of force development in the limited studies that measured it. The authors also noted that finger strength was the more commonly reported outcome, so evidence for dynamic qualities remains narrower than many training claims suggest. Read the [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9806751/) as support for targeted training—not proof that a bigger test number automatically creates better dyno technique.

## Measure the Limiter, Not the Spectacle

If a dyno keeps failing, sort the cause before adding exercises:

- **You cannot reach the zone:** examine foot drive, hip path, and timing.
- **You touch but cannot hold:** examine contact accuracy, hold direction, finger force, and body swing.
- **You catch but swing off:** examine shoulder control, core tension, and foot recovery.
- **Your first try is good and later attempts collapse:** examine rest and session volume.
- **Fear changes the takeoff:** reduce the move, improve the landing, and rebuild confidence gradually.

Video from a safe angle can reveal the trajectory. A training log can show attempt quality and rest. A finger-testing system can describe finger qualities, but it cannot measure your route reading, commitment, or hip path.

## A 20-Minute Dyno Practice Session

Use this after a general [climbing warm-up](/blog/climbing-warm-up-routine/) and before fatigue-heavy projecting.

1. **Five minutes:** easy climbs with deliberate leg drive and soft catches.
2. **Five minutes:** controlled rock-and-release drills on large holds.
3. **Six minutes:** three to five high-quality attempts on one modest dyno, with full rest.
4. **Four minutes:** link an easier dynamic move into a complete problem.

Finish while the movement still looks good. Do not add campus-board work automatically; it creates additional high-intensity finger and shoulder load. If campus training is already appropriate for you, place it deliberately using the [campus board workout guide](/blog/campus-board-workout/) and the [recovery planner](/tools/training-load-recovery-planner/).

## FAQ

### What does dyno mean in climbing?

“Dyno” is short for dynamic movement. In common gym use, it usually means a move where momentum—and often a moment with the feet or whole body off the wall—carries the climber to the next hold.

### Are dynos only for advanced climbers?

No. Beginners can practice very small dynamic movements on large holds and good mats. Large coordination jumps, poor landings, and high-impact catches should wait until the foundation is reliable.

### Should I train dynos on a campus board?

Not by default. A campus board loads the fingers and upper body intensely and removes the foot-driven technique central to many dynos. Practice dynamic movement on easy climbs first.

### How often should I practice dynos?

Short, fresh exposures once or twice a week can be enough to learn timing. The right frequency depends on the rest of your bouldering, finger, and strength load.

### Why can I touch the hold but not stick it?

You may arrive too fast, contact the wrong part of the hold, swing away from the wall, or lack enough force for that grip. Reduce distance and isolate which part of the catch fails.

## Bottom Line

Better dynos come from better coordination, not bigger leaps. Use the feet, aim the hips, watch the target, catch near the deadpoint, and land under control. Practice on easy terrain while fresh.

Then measure only what matters. Technique problems need technique work. If finger force is the unresolved limiter, use a repeatable [grip strength test for climbers](/blog/grip-strength-test-climbers-climbro/) before adding random high-intensity training.
