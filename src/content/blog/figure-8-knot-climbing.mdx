The figure 8 follow-through is the standard tie-in knot taught in many climbing gyms. You form a loose figure eight in the rope, pass the working end through the harness's designated tie-in points, retrace the original knot, dress the strands neatly, and leave the tail required by your gym or instructor.

That sentence is a reminder, not a substitute for hands-on instruction. Learn the knot from a qualified instructor, follow your harness and gym rules, and complete a partner check before every climb.

## Quick Answer: How Do You Tie a Figure 8 Knot for Climbing?

1. Form a bight and make a loose figure eight in the rope.
2. Pass the working end through the harness tie-in points specified by the harness manufacturer.
3. Feed the working end back through the original knot, following the same path in reverse.
4. Dress the knot so paired strands lie neatly beside each other without unintended crossings.
5. Tighten each strand and confirm the tail meets your facility's rule.
6. Have your partner inspect the knot, harness, belay setup, and rope before climbing.

Petzl's official [gym-climbing preparation guide](https://www.petzl.com/sfc/servlet.shepherd/version/download/0681r00000718S5AAI) illustrates the tying sequence. Use the instructions supplied with your own harness and rope as the controlling product guidance.

## Figure 8 on a Bight vs Figure 8 Follow-Through

The names sound similar, but the tasks differ.

**Figure eight on a bight:** tied in a doubled section of rope, creating a fixed loop without using the rope end to retrace the knot.

**Figure eight follow-through:** starts with a loose figure eight, then sends the rope end through the harness tie-in points and retraces the knot. This is the version commonly used to attach a climber directly to the rope.

Do not rely on the final silhouette alone. Know which construction the situation requires and why the rope passes where it does.

## How to Recognize a Well-Dressed Knot

A well-dressed figure eight is compact and easy to inspect. The retraced strands run side by side around the knot rather than crossing unpredictably. Both climbers can follow the rope from the loaded strand, through the knot and harness, to the tail.

Use a consistent visual check:

- **Eight shape:** the knot has the expected figure-eight structure.
- **Retrace:** the working end follows the original path all the way around.
- **Harness path:** the rope passes through the tie-in points required by the harness instructions and facility.
- **Dress:** paired strands sit neatly, without hidden loops or partial retracing.
- **Tightness:** each strand has been individually snugged so the knot is compact.
- **Tail:** enough tail remains for the rule taught by your instructor or gym.

The UIAA Safety Commission notes that figure-eight variants can both function when correctly formed and emphasizes the clamping geometry of the loaded rope in its [figure-eight knot guidance](https://www.theuiaa.org/uiaa-safecom-answers-your-questions-figure-eight-knots/). For everyday gym use, consistency and inspection matter more than arguing over a preferred visual variation.

## Common Figure 8 Knot Mistakes

### Tying through the wrong point

A harness may have belay loop, tie-in points, gear loops, and adjustment hardware. They do not serve the same purpose. Follow the harness instructions and the facility's teaching; never choose a connection point by appearance.

### Incomplete retracing

The working end must follow the original figure eight completely. Stopping early can leave a knot that looks busy but is not the intended tie-in.

### Hidden crossings and loose sections

Messy strands make inspection harder. Dress the knot before loading it, then snug each strand. If either partner cannot trace the rope path confidently, untie it and start again.

### Tail that does not meet the local rule

Different organizations and instructors may teach a minimum tail or a specific backup-knot practice. Follow the rule at the facility where you are climbing. Do not improvise a shorter tail because the knot “looks finished.”

### Skipping the partner check

Familiarity creates shortcuts. The hundredth tie-in deserves the same check as the first. A partner check is not an insult to experience; it is a deliberate interruption before leaving the ground.

## The Pre-Climb Partner Check

Both climbers should participate. Point to each item rather than giving the system a quick glance.

For the climber:

- harness is on correctly and adjusted
- rope passes through the required tie-in points
- knot is complete, dressed, tightened, and has the required tail

For the belayer:

- harness is on correctly and adjusted
- device and connector are compatible and oriented according to their instructions
- locking connector is closed and locked where required
- brake strand is identified and controlled

For the system:

- climber and belayer agree on the plan and commands
- the correct rope is in the system
- a stopper knot or other rope-end control is used when required
- route length and lowering plan fit the rope and facility setup

If you are preparing for a facility assessment, read [what to expect in a climbing gym belay test](/blog/belay-test-climbing-gym/). Gym rules differ, so practice the exact system your facility teaches.

## How to Practice the Figure 8 Safely

Practice at ground level with an instructor or experienced, facility-approved partner. Tie slowly enough to name each step. Then untie and repeat from the beginning rather than memorizing one finished knot.

A useful practice sequence is:

1. Tie the initial figure eight until its orientation is familiar.
2. Trace the rope through the harness points under supervision.
3. Retrace the knot while keeping the strands paired.
4. Dress, tighten, and perform the same verbal check every time.
5. Swap roles and inspect another person's knot.

Do not learn by loading an unverified knot. Videos and diagrams can refresh the sequence, but a qualified person should confirm your system in person.

## When the Figure 8 Is Used Indoors

The follow-through is commonly used for tying directly into the rope for [top-rope climbing](/blog/top-rope-climbing-guide/) and [lead climbing](/blog/lead-climbing-guide/). Some gyms use facility-specific top-rope attachment systems, so follow staff instructions rather than assuming every lane requires the same setup.

It is not the knot used for every job. Anchors, rescue systems, fixed loops, joining ropes, and accessory cord can require other knots and more advanced instruction. This guide covers the direct climber tie-in only.

## FAQ

### Is the figure 8 knot hard to untie after a fall?

It can tighten after repeated or heavy loading. Proper dressing helps make the structure easier to inspect and work loose, but you should not modify the knot casually to make it easier to untie.

### Do I need a backup knot?

Follow your gym, instructor, and equipment guidance. Practices differ, and the required tail and backup method should be taught as part of one consistent system.

### Can I tie into the belay loop?

Use the tie-in points specified by your harness manufacturer and gym. Do not substitute the belay loop unless the product instructions explicitly direct that use for the situation.

### How long should the tail be?

Use the minimum taught by your facility or instructor and shown in the relevant equipment guidance. A tail should be easy for both partners to inspect; guessing a universal length is less useful than following the system you were taught.

### Is a figure 8 knot enough for lead climbing?

The knot attaches the climber to the rope. Lead climbing also requires competent belaying, clipping, fall management, communication, compatible equipment, and facility approval. Take a lead class and pass the gym's assessment.

## Bottom Line

A climbing figure 8 is safe only as part of a checked system. Tie the follow-through through the correct harness points, retrace it completely, dress and tighten the strands, keep the required tail, and let your partner inspect it.

If you are still learning the rope system, find a facility through the [indoor climbing gym directory](/search/) and take a class. The fastest useful knot is the one you can tie correctly, explain clearly, and check every time.
