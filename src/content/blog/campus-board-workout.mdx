A campus board workout uses footless movement on evenly spaced rungs to train climbing-specific pulling power, contact strength, reach, and coordination. It is high-intensity training for experienced climbers—not a beginner shortcut.

Start with simple matched ladders, use large rungs, rest several minutes between quality sets, and stop before speed or accuracy falls apart. If you cannot move smoothly on large rungs, keep your feet on the wall and train the movement pattern first.

## Quick Campus Board Workout

For an experienced, pain-free adult climber who already has a base of finger and pulling strength:

1. Warm up with 15–20 minutes of easy climbing and progressive pulls.
2. Use the largest comfortable campus rungs.
3. Perform a controlled ladder: rung 1 to 2 to 3, match, then step down.
4. Complete 3–5 quality sets.
5. Rest 2–4 minutes between sets.
6. Stop when you miss, swing excessively, catch with poor shoulder position, or feel sharp discomfort.
7. Allow recovery before the next hard finger or bouldering session.

This is a template, not a universal prescription. The campus-board study with the clearest direct training comparison included only 16 advanced-to-elite male climbers who were already familiar with intense finger training. Do not copy an elite protocol simply because the rungs are available.

## What a Campus Board Trains

A campus board is a steep panel with horizontal rungs. Climbers move between the rungs without using their feet.

That makes it different from a hangboard.

- **Hangboard training** is mostly isometric: you hold a position on an edge.
- **Campus board training** is dynamic: you produce force quickly, move the body, and catch the next rung.
- **Wall climbing** adds feet, route decisions, varied holds, body positions, and technique.

The campus board is specific, but it is not complete. It does not replace climbing.

A five-week randomized study of [two versus four weekly campus-board sessions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34267583/) found improvements in several climbing-specific measures when the training groups were combined, including rung strength, bouldering performance, maximal reach, and moves to failure. The researchers also reported no overall differences between the two training-frequency groups across most comparisons.

That is encouraging evidence, but the sample was small and highly trained. A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9806751/) likewise found that finger- and campus-board training can improve climbing-specific strength measures while stressing the need for more high-quality research.

The practical takeaway: campus training can work, but more sessions are not automatically better.

## Are You Ready for a Campus Board Workout?

Use the readiness check before choosing exercises.

### Better signs of readiness

- You have several years of consistent climbing, not several weeks.
- You can climb hard while keeping shoulders engaged and feet accurate.
- You already tolerate direct finger-strength work.
- You recover reliably from limit bouldering.
- You can control a large-rung ladder without wild swinging.
- A coach or experienced climber has reviewed your form.

### Reasons to delay campus training

- You are new to climbing.
- Your fingers, elbows, or shoulders are currently painful.
- You are a youth climber without qualified coaching and age-appropriate guidance.
- You cannot hold the large rungs with control.
- You already stack hard bouldering, max hangs, and pulling work into the same week.
- You want campus training only because stronger climbers use it.

For most newer climbers, [climbing technique practice](/blog/how-to-improve-climbing-technique/) and deliberate [footwork drills](/blog/climbing-footwork-drills/) offer a better return with less concentrated finger load.

## The Warm-Up

Do not walk from the front desk to the campus board.

Use a progressive warm-up:

1. **Raise temperature:** easy movement for 5–10 minutes.
2. **Climb easy terrain:** choose large holds and varied movement.
3. **Prepare the fingers:** controlled submaximal hangs or feet-supported pulls on large edges.
4. **Prepare the shoulders:** easy scapular pulls and controlled pull-up positions.
5. **Rehearse with feet:** touch the campus rungs while keeping feet on footholds.
6. **Test one easy ladder:** stop if the first set feels awkward or unusually hard.

Our full [climbing warm-up routine](/blog/climbing-warm-up-routine/) covers the general sequence. The campus-specific part should narrow from whole-body movement to the exact rung and reach pattern you plan to train.

## Campus Board Workout 1: Controlled Ladders

This is the best starting exercise for experienced climbers new to campus training.

### Setup

- Use large rungs.
- Start matched on the lowest comfortable rung.
- Keep the shoulders active rather than hanging passively.
- Use a small, controlled swing—not a violent kip.

### Movement

Move one hand to the next rung. Bring the other hand up to match. Continue for two or three moves, match, and step down safely.

Example:

- both hands on rung 1
- right hand to rung 2
- left hand matches rung 2
- left hand to rung 3
- right hand matches rung 3
- step down

Alternate the leading hand each set.

### Dose

Perform 3–5 sets with 2–4 minutes of rest. End the workout when catches become loud, shoulders rise toward the ears, or accuracy falls.

The purpose is repeatable force. Not exhaustion.

## Campus Board Workout 2: Alternating Ladders

Use this after matched ladders feel controlled.

Instead of matching each rung, alternate hands continuously:

- right hand to rung 2
- left hand to rung 3
- right hand to rung 4

Keep the rung spacing conservative. Reach only as far as you can catch with control.

Try 3–4 sets per leading side. Rest 3 minutes or longer between sets.

This variation demands more rhythm and body control. If you twist, cut sideways, or slap at the rung, reduce the spacing or return to matched ladders.

## Campus Board Workout 3: Max Reach Practice

Max-reach work is more intense and should not be the first campus exercise you learn.

Start matched on a comfortable rung. Move one hand upward as far as you can while still catching the target solidly. Match or step down. Take full rest before the next attempt.

Use a low attempt count:

- 2–3 attempts per side
- 3–5 minutes of rest
- no repeated misses

Do not chase a higher rung by letting the shoulder collapse or barely touching the edge. A lower clean catch is more useful than a higher uncontrolled one.

## Why We Skip Double Dynos in the Starter Plan

A double dyno sends both hands to a higher rung at the same time. It looks like the defining campus-board move, which is exactly why people rush into it.

It also removes one of your points of control and raises the demand on the catch.

Earn it later. Build accurate ladders, alternating movement, and shoulder control first. If a qualified coach adds double dynos, use large rungs, modest spacing, low volume, and full rest.

Youth climbers need extra caution. Research on adolescent climbing safety notes that finger growth-plate injuries are a key youth concern, and high-intensity campus-board choices require age-appropriate guidance. Do not turn an adult internet workout into a youth training plan.

## A Four-Week Campus Board Progression

Keep one variable moving at a time.

| Week | Focus | Sets |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Large-rung matched ladders | 3 |
| 2 | Same ladder, cleaner rhythm | 4 |
| 3 | Add one alternating-ladder set | 3 matched + 1 alternating |
| 4 | Deload or repeat the easiest successful week | 2–3 |

Do not shrink the rungs, increase reach, add sets, and reduce rest in the same week. That makes it impossible to tell which change exceeded your capacity.

Record:

- rung size
- move sequence
- leading hand
- successful sets
- rest time
- quality of each catch
- next-day finger, elbow, and shoulder response

The [training-load recovery planner](/tools/training-load-recovery-planner/) can help you see where the session sits beside bouldering, rope climbing, and strength work.

## Where Campus Training Fits in a Climbing Week

Campus work belongs near the high-intensity end of the week.

Do it while fresh, not after two hours of projecting. Avoid placing it next to another maximal finger session until you know how you recover. Many climbers should start with one short campus session per week, then judge the effect on climbing quality.

A simple structure might be:

- **Day 1:** technique and moderate climbing
- **Day 2:** rest or easy recovery
- **Day 3:** short campus workout, then limited quality bouldering
- **Day 4:** rest
- **Day 5:** rope volume or easier climbing

Use [How to Structure Your Climbing Week](/blog/how-to-structure-climbing-week-strength-technique-recovery/) for the bigger picture. If the real weakness is sustaining moves while pumped, [power-endurance training](/blog/climbing-power-endurance-training/) may fit better than campus ladders.

## Common Campus Board Mistakes

### Using rungs that are too small

Smaller rungs raise finger demand and often make movement worse. Start large enough to move well.

### Training to failure

Campus work is usually about high-quality force and coordination. Sets that end in repeated misses rehearse poor catches and add fatigue.

### Ignoring the shoulders

Keep the shoulder girdle active and the movement controlled. If you cannot maintain position, reduce the difficulty.

### Adding campus work to an already hard week

The new workout is not free. Replace or reduce another high-intensity session instead of stacking everything.

### Copying an elite protocol

The direct training study involved advanced-to-elite climbers familiar with intense finger training. Its results do not prove that the same frequency or volume is appropriate for a recreational beginner.

## FAQ

### What is a campus board workout good for?

Campus workouts can train dynamic climbing-specific pulling, contact strength, reach, and movement coordination on rungs. They are most appropriate for experienced climbers with an existing strength base.

### How often should you campus board?

Many climbers should start with one short session per week. Training frequency depends on experience, total climbing load, recovery, and the quality of each session.

### Should beginners use a campus board?

Most beginners should prioritize climbing, technique, footwork, and general strength. Feet-on rung drills can teach movement without turning the exercise into full campus training.

### Is a campus board the same as a hangboard?

No. Hangboarding is mainly isometric holding. Campus boarding uses dynamic footless moves and catches, involving the fingers and the wider pulling chain.

### Should campus-board training hurt?

No. Stop for sharp pain or unusual discomfort. Do not use a generic article to diagnose or train through an injury; consult an appropriate healthcare professional when needed.

## Bottom Line

A useful campus board workout is short, controlled, and earned.

Start on large rungs. Use simple ladders. Rest long enough to preserve accurate catches. Add only one progression at a time, and judge the session by what it does to the rest of your climbing week.

If you are not ready for footless rung work, that is not a failure. Use [climbing strength training](/blog/climbing-strength-training/) and technique sessions to build the base first.
