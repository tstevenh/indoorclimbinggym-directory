You are 90 minutes into a session at the bouldering gym, your forearms are pumped, and the bottle you filled at the front desk is still close to full in your bag. Climbing comes in short efforts separated by long rests, so it is easy to go an hour without drinking. In a controlled study of climbing dehydration, experienced climbers who did not drink lost 2.4% of their body mass in under 2 hours.

## Fluid Loss in a 2-Hour Session

Brian Conder at Montana State University tested 10 experienced climbers on a motorized indoor climbing treadmill in 2011. Each climber completed 2 identical 115-minute trials made of 6 climbing intervals of 15 minutes, with 5 minutes of rest between them. In the dry trial they had no water. In the other, they drank as much as they wanted during the rests.

Without water, body mass fell by 2.4% on average. With water available, the loss was 0.9%, so even climbers who could drink freely finished the session with a small deficit. Plasma volume dropped by about 3% in the no-water trial and rose by close to 9% when the climbers drank, and heart rate stayed lower when water was available. Urine specific gravity at the end of the trial did not pick up the dehydration that the scale recorded, which means a quick look at urine color after a session can mislead a climber about how much they lost.

## How Much Water to Drink During Indoor Climbing

General exercise guidance from the American College of Sports Medicine suggests approximately 0.4 to 0.8 L of fluid per hour, with smaller and slower athletes at the low end and larger, harder-working athletes at the high end. A 2-hour climbing session therefore gives a broad planning range of 0.8 to 1.6 L, although individual needs may be lower or higher depending on sweat rate, gym temperature, and exercise intensity. Spread across the natural rests between problems or routes, drinking roughly 150 to 250 mL at regular intervals can be a practical approach, adjusted to thirst and personal fluid losses. A bottle left at the base of the wall, in sight between attempts, gets drunk far more reliably than one zipped inside a bag.

Before the session, the ACSM suggests 5 to 7 mL per kilogram of body weight about 4 hours before exercise, which is 350 to 490 mL for a 70 kg climber. A climber whose urine is still dark 2 hours before the session can add another 3 to 5 mL per kilogram, provided there is no reason to restrict fluid intake.

## Long Sessions and Warm Gyms

Sweat contains sodium as well as water, and the amount lost grows with session length and gym temperature. A 70 kg climber who loses 2.4% of body mass is down about 1.7 L, and in a 3-hour session on a hot summer evening the loss can be larger. Climbers on 3-hour sessions in warm gyms can <a href="https://thefeed.com/hydration" target="_blank" rel="noopener">use electrolyte powders</a> in 1 bottle and keep plain water in the other. Shorter bouldering sessions of 60 to 90 minutes generally need nothing beyond water, depending on individual sweat losses and conditions.

Electrolytes can help replace some sodium lost through sweating, but drinking excessively should still be avoided, even when using electrolyte drinks.

## Heart Rate on the Wall

Climbing raises heart rate more than its stop-and-start pattern suggests. In a 1997 study of indoor rock climbing with 14 experienced climbers, Christine Mermier and colleagues measured heart rates of 74% to 85% of predicted maximum on walls ranging from vertical to a horizontal roof. The energy expenditure in those trials was similar to running at 8 to 11 minutes per mile. Blood lactate and oxygen use rose along with heart rate as the wall got steeper.

The authors also found that heart rate and oxygen use did not rise together in the steady line seen in running or cycling. Repeated isometric gripping by the forearms raises heart rate out of proportion to oxygen use, so heart rate alone is a poor guide to aerobic effort during a hard problem.

## Grip Strength and Fluid Loss

Dehydration can affect muscular endurance more than maximal strength. A 2007 review led by Daniel Judelson put the drop at about 2% for strength and 3% for power, against about 10% for high-intensity endurance, which can matter on a long boulder problem or a pumpy route. Single maximal efforts held up better in Julien Périard's work, where dehydration of about 2.5% without heat stress left single-contraction strength unchanged.

Conder's no-water trial did produce higher heart rates, so each attempt in a dehydrated session placed more strain on the cardiovascular system.

Among 13 male competitors measured at a 2004 IFSC World Cup event, climbers who finished in the top 30 had more total body water than those who placed lower. This suggests a possible association between body water and competition results, although it does not establish that drinking more directly improves climbing performance.

## Chalk and Sweaty Hands

Dry hands are a separate question from hydration, and the lab evidence on grip comes from [3 chalk studies](https://www.vertigemedia.fr/en/chalk-friction-humidity-rock-type-study) with different answers. A 2001 laboratory test found that chalk lowered fingertip friction on a flat rock sample. A 2012 hangboard study with 11 climbers found that chalk improved grip on limestone and sandstone, while a 2024 University of Sheffield study found that the effect depended on humidity and rock texture.

These findings show that managing sweaty hands and maintaining adequate hydration are different parts of climbing preparation. For practical options, see our guide to [the best climbing chalk for sweaty hands](/blog/best-climbing-chalk-for-sweaty-hands/).

## Outdoor Days on Real Rock

Fluid needs can rise sharply once the session moves outside in summer. The American Alpine Club, in its 2002 accident report, described a June case of [exhaustion on Moore's Wall](https://publications.americanalpineclub.org/articles/13200205400/Inadequate-WaterDehydration-Exhaustion-North-Carolina-Moores-Wall-Sentinel-Buttress) in North Carolina, where a 19-year-old climber collapsed from dehydration about 50 feet from the top of a route at Sentinel Buttress. He had brought half a can of soda for the whole day. The report noted that climbers in hot, humid weather can lose about 1 L of sweat in an hour of exertion, and the rescue took 26 people roughly 5.5 hours each. Its advice emphasized drinking before exercise and regularly during activity, with fluid intake adjusted to conditions and individual needs.

## Daily Water Between Sessions

Session drinking is part of a person's daily water needs, which differ widely. A 2022 study in *Science* measured [daily water turnover](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/11/221125132035.htm) in more than 5,600 people from 26 countries and found typical values from about 1 to 6 L. A 20-year-old man of average activity weighing 70 kg turned over about 3.2 L a day, compared with about 2.7 L for a woman of the same age weighing 60 kg. Athletes turned over about 1 L more than non-athletes, and doubling energy expenditure added roughly 1 L to the expected daily figure.

These figures describe total water turnover, including water obtained from food and produced through metabolism, rather than the amount a person needs to drink. Someone who climbs 3 evenings a week may need more total water on those days than on rest days, depending on activity and sweat losses. The authors also challenged the idea that everyone needs exactly 8 glasses of 8 ounces of water each day.

## The Answer in Milliliters

A climber in a temperate gym for 2 hours can use roughly 0.8 to 1.6 L of fluid as a general planning range, adjusting the amount to thirst, sweat losses, and session intensity rather than treating it as a fixed requirement. Drinking approximately 150 to 250 mL during suitable rest breaks can make hydration easier to manage, while around 400 mL a few hours before arriving may help a climber start the session adequately hydrated.

Conder's climbers who drank freely during their rests finished within 1% of their starting weight, showing the value of having water available throughout the session. The goal is not to empty a certain number of bottles but to replace fluid sensibly without drinking more than the body needs.
